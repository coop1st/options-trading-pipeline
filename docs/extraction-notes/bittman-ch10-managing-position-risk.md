Source: Bittman, *Trading Options as a Professional*, Chapter 10 "Managing Position Risk", printed pp. 311–340, plus Epilogue, printed p. 343.

## Calculating Position Risks (p.312–314, Table 10-1)

- **Position-Greek formula** (generalizing Ch.8's position-delta method to all Greeks): `position risk = individual-option Greek × multiplier (100) × number of contracts`, summed across every leg for a multi-part position. This chapter covers delta/gamma/vega/theta risk; **rho is explicitly excluded** since small short-term interest-rate moves don't materially affect short-term option positions.
- Worked example (Table 10-1): 20 long 70 Calls (2.82 each, delta 0.535, gamma 0.059, vega 0.087, theta −0.310, 7-day theta) → **position price $5,640; position delta 1,070; position gamma 118; position vega 174; position theta −620** (loses $620 over the next 7 days if nothing else changes).
- Interpreting each: **position delta** = share-equivalent exposure (a $1 stock move ≈ ±$1,070 P&L). **Position gamma** = how much position delta itself shifts per $1 stock move (a $1 rise moves delta 1,070→1,188). **Position vega** = $ P&L per 1-point IV change ($174). **Position theta** = $ P&L per "one unit" of time (here, 7 days = −$620).
- **Vega risk is looser than theta risk**: vega tells you the $-impact of a 1-point IV move, but not the likelihood or typical magnitude of such a move — that requires separately studying historic/implied volatility context (Ch.7); "forecasting volatility is an art, not a science." Theta, by contrast, gives a firm, near-certain estimate (time passes at a known, fixed rate) — useful for combining with a dollar risk limit to decide how long to hold a position before taking a decay-driven loss.
- **Risks of Short Options** (p.314): Greeks of long and short options are mathematically opposite, but the *practical risk profile* isn't simply mirrored — an **uncovered short option** (no offsetting position that caps risk) carries **unlimited risk for short calls** and **substantial (but bounded, since price can't go below zero) risk for short puts**. Real markets can gap 30%+ overnight on unexpected news, so a short option's realistic risk exceeds simple modeled assumptions. No uniform rule exists for "how many short options is too many" — an individual, subjective judgment.

## Managing Directional Risk with Delta (p.314–318, Table 10-2 — "Grace")

- **Technique**: since stock prices tend to move in a choppy up-and-down pattern rather than a straight line, and long options have positive gamma (delta rises as price rises, falls as price falls), a trader can **sell part of a long-call position when a rally pushes delta above a threshold, and buy back when a pullback drops delta below a lower threshold** — aiming to keep position delta roughly centered around a target level throughout the trade, capturing extra profit from the natural oscillation.
- **Sizing the adjustment**: `contracts to trade = |target delta − current delta| / (option delta × 100)`.
- Worked example: Grace targets ~1,100 position delta (starting 20 long 70 Calls ≈ 1,060), with sell-trigger at ≥1,500 and buy-trigger at ≤900. Over a 16-day, 6-price-point walk (70→76→72→77→73→78), she sells/buys calls at each trigger breach, finishing by selling the remaining 15 calls at $8.27. **Result: $12,625 profit** (excluding costs) vs. **$10,900 from a naive buy-and-hold** of the original 20 calls (buy @2.82, hold, sell @8.27 at the end) — a **$1,725 improvement** from actively managing delta through the same overall price path.
- **Important caveat**: this outperformance depends on the stock's path being genuinely choppy/volatile — "it tends to underperform the buy-and-hold approach when prices trend with below-average volatility," and it is not risk-free: if the stock had fallen sharply right after the initial purchase (delta dropping below 900), the rules would have forced Grace to buy *more* calls into a losing position, and a total loss could exceed the original $5,640 investment if everything ultimately expired worthless.

## Tracking Changes in Position Risk (p.318–320, Table 10-3)

- **Vertical spreads carry meaningfully lower Greeks across the board than the equivalent outright long option.** Worked comparison (20 long 70-75 bull call spreads, same 70 stock/35-day/31%-vol/4%-rate assumptions as Table 10-1's outright calls): position price $3,600 (vs. $5,640 outright); delta 546 (vs. 1,070); gamma 20 (vs. 118); vega 42 (vs. 174); theta −170 (vs. −620). **Every Greek is reduced** by adding the short higher-strike call leg — the spread is structurally less sensitive to price, time, and volatility changes than the naked long call.

### Vertical Spreads versus Outright Long Options (p.320–321, Table 10-4)

- Worked 2-scenario comparison of a long 70 Call vs. the 70-75 call spread, both starting at stock=70/35 days/31% IV: **Scenario 1** (stock rallies to 73.50, 14 days left, IV unchanged at 31%) → call profits 1.29, spread profits 1.12 (call wins). **Scenario 2** (identical price/time move, but IV *drops* to 24%) → call profits only 1.03 (hurt by falling IV), while the spread profits *more*, 1.25 (partially insulated from the IV drop, since its short leg's IV exposure offsets the long leg's). **Conclusion: vertical spreads can outperform outright long options specifically in declining-implied-volatility environments**, because they're less vega-sensitive.

### Vertical Spreads — How the Risks Change (p.321–323, Table 10-5, Figs 10-1–10-5)

- Position-risk snapshots are only valid at one instant — the same 70-75 bull call spread's Greeks **completely reverse character** as the stock price moves from 70 (long leg ATM) to 75 (short leg ATM): at stock=70 (Table 10-3) → delta 546, gamma +20, vega +42, theta −170 (a *bullish, long-volatility, time-decay-hurts* profile). At stock=75 (Table 10-5) → delta 512 (still positive but the position's character has changed), gamma **−30** (now negative), vega **−31** (now negative), theta **+90** (now positive) — a *bullish-but-now-time-decay-helps, volatility-decline-helps* profile. **A $5 stock move flipped gamma, vega, and theta from positive to negative (or vice versa) while delta stayed directionally the same** — meaning the position's *primary source of expected profit* shifted from "further stock rally" toward "time simply passing with the stock near the short strike."
- **Graphed risk curves** (Figs 10-1–10-5, for 20 long 70-75 spreads across a stock-price range at 35 vs. 17 days to expiration): value rises from near-zero (stock below 70) to max value (stock above 75); delta peaks between the strikes and goes to 0 (stock<70) or 2,000/full stock-equivalent (stock between 70-75 at expiration, since the long calls get exercised); gamma is positive near/below the lower strike (long call effect) and flips negative near the upper strike (short call effect) as expiration nears; vega follows a similar positive-then-negative shape but the 35-day/17-day curves stay proportionally closer together than gamma's (since vega decays similarly for ATM and OTM options, whereas gamma diverges more by moneyness as expiration nears — per Ch.4); theta is close to a mirror image of gamma/vega (negative near the long strike, positive near the short strike). **Practical lesson**: traders should use software (Op-Eval Pro) to graph these curves regularly, since "if you do not know how your risks have changed, you cannot react to changing market conditions within your pre-established limits."

## Greeks of Delta-Neutral Positions (p.327–329, Table 10-6, Fig 10-6)

- Taking the 20 long 70-75 spreads and adding 546 short shares to zero out delta reveals three things: (1) the position's **stated dollar "price"** (net credit from the short stock minus net debit for the options, −$34,620 here) is **not** a true risk measure, since the short-stock leg alone carries theoretically unlimited upside risk; (2) being delta-neutral **only protects against price moves within a finite range** (here, roughly stock=68–76 for the 35-day curve) — outside that range the position starts to lose meaningfully, so delta-neutral ≠ risk-free, just risk-*reduced-within-a-band*; (3) **gamma, vega, and theta are completely unaffected by adding the stock hedge** (identical to the un-hedged spread's values in Table 10-3) — going delta-neutral does nothing to manage those other three risks, which must be addressed separately.

## Neutralizing Position Greeks (p.329–337)

- **Sizing a neutralizing trade**: `contracts needed = position Greek being neutralized / that option's individual Greek` (sign determines buy vs. sell — sell options to reduce a positive position Greek, buy options to reduce a negative one), then add/trim stock to restore delta-neutrality given the option trade's own delta impact.

### Zero-interest-rate case ("Matthew," Tables 10-7A–C)

- Initial 4-leg position (short 80 Puts, short 85 Calls, long 90 Calls, short stock) has delta≈0 but gamma +104, vega +358, theta −780 — profits from a big move or rising IV, loses to time decay.
- **Key finding**: neutralizing *any one* of gamma, vega, or theta by trading the 85 Calls (plus an offsetting stock trade to preserve delta-neutrality) **requires the identical quantity (28 contracts) in all three cases** — i.e., **when interest rates are zero, neutralizing any one of gamma/vega/theta automatically neutralizes the other two as well.** Confirmed by recomputing the full Greek set post-trade: all four Greeks land at ≈0 (small residuals from rounding).
- Practical implication: with zero rates (this scenario "applies generally to options on futures"), a trader doesn't need to choose which Greek to target — they're all linked.

### Positive-interest-rate case ("Matthew" again, Tables 10-8A–C)

- Same setup style but with a 5% rate: neutralizing gamma or vega (via buying 60 Puts) again requires the *same* quantity (15 contracts) for both — **gamma and vega remain linked even with positive rates**. But **neutralizing theta requires a different quantity (27 contracts)** — the link between theta and the other two Greeks breaks once carrying costs (interest) enter the picture.
- **Why**: when a position carries a large stock component, the resulting net debit or credit accrues real interest cost/income. The example shows the neutralized (gamma/vega-zeroed) position's residual theta of +$146/week is explained almost exactly by the **interest cost of financing the position's $158,300 net debit** (158,300 × 5% × 7/365 ≈ $152, matching the $146 theta within rounding) — i.e., **theta ends up absorbing/reflecting the position's financing cost/benefit rather than being independently neutralizable alongside gamma/vega.** A positive theta offsets a debit position's interest expense; a negative theta would offset a credit position's interest income.
- **This interest-theta linkage only applies when the position has a meaningful stock component** — for stock-free option combinations, theta must instead be reasoned about via its usual inverse relationship with gamma/vega (same-expiration positions with positive gamma+vega always have negative theta, and vice versa).

## Establishing Risk Limits (p.337–340)

- No "scientific" universal risk limit exists — the foundational, inherently subjective first question every trader must answer is **"how much of an adverse dollar (or %) swing can I tolerate and still trade rationally?"** Everything else (position sizing, Greek limits) follows from that personal answer.
- **Three position types, three risk-limit approaches**:
  1. **Delta-neutral with a stock component**: focus risk limits on **either gamma or vega** (not both — they're linked, per the zero/positive-rate findings above) since theta "takes care of itself" via the interest relationship. Method: judge a "normal" IV move size from historic/implied vol data (Ch.7), multiply by the position's vega to get a "normal" dollar swing, and compare that against your chosen dollar risk limit to size an acceptable vega. Worked example: if $1,000 is your limit and a 3-point IV move is judged "normal," your vega limit is 1,000/3 ≈ **333** (so a position with vega ±143, per Table 10-8A, is within a $1,000 limit unless IV swings a much-larger-than-normal 7 points).
  2. **Delta-neutral without a stock component**: theta can't be tied to an interest calculation here, but it *is* still mechanically tied to gamma/vega (opposite sign, same-expiration options) — so the real decision is a strategic one: "do I want to be a net option buyer (profit from a big move/rising IV, pay theta) or a net option seller (profit from time decay, risk a big move/rising IV)?" Once that's decided, set a limit on vega or theta accordingly.
  3. **Directional positions**: the dominant risk is **delta** — "how much delta can I take on?" is the first question, answered via individually-chosen **stop-loss points** (expressed in $ amount, option price, or underlying price) set below the theoretical max loss, since few traders want to risk 100% of a long option's premium, and since a losing short-option position's negative gamma makes losses accelerate as the market moves further against it (making stop-losses especially critical for short-option directional trades). Attempting to also manage gamma/vega/theta on a directional trade usually just perturbs delta further (e.g., selling a higher-strike call to cut vega risk on existing long calls also cuts delta — the very thing the trade was meant to preserve) — so directional risk management should stay centered on delta first and treat other-Greek adjustments as secondary/often not worth the delta side-effects.

## Summary

Managing position risk blends objective calculation (position Greeks = sum of quantity-weighted individual-option Greeks) with unavoidably subjective judgment (how much risk is tolerable, what counts as a "normal" volatility move, where to set stop-losses). Every strategy — outright options, verticals, ratios, and more — trades off profit potential against risk differently, and those risk profiles are not static: they shift, sometimes reversing sign entirely, as price, time, and volatility change (as the 70-vs-75 stock-price bull-spread example showed). Directional traders should manage primarily via delta (sometimes actively trimming/adding to a winning long-option position to lock in gains from natural price oscillation); delta-neutral traders must separately manage gamma/vega (which move together) and theta (linked to financing cost when a stock leg is present, or to gamma/vega's sign when it isn't). No Greek-based calculation replaces the initial, purely personal decision of how large a loss a trader can tolerate.

## Epilogue (printed p.343)

Closing reflection: computers assist market makers with execution, pricing, and risk monitoring, but do not replace human judgment — professional traders (market makers and advanced individual traders alike) still need genuine mastery of option price behavior, volatility, synthetic relationships/arbitrage, delta-neutral trading, bid-ask pricing, and position-risk management (i.e., the book's own eight-essentials structure from the Introduction). For individual (non-market-maker) traders, the value of understanding how market makers think is to sharpen order-entry and strategy-performance judgment — market makers are a distinct kind of market participant (not competing directional speculators, and only one participant among many, not "the market" itself), who take on and are compensated for genuine risk just as any trader is. Market forecasting remains an art, but a solid grounding in volatility should help with estimating price ranges, picking targets, and anticipating strategy performance — success ultimately requires objectivity over emotion and consistent discipline in applying whatever technique a trader chooses.
