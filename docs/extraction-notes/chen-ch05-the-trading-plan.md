## Overview / Introduction (p.72)

Opens with Alan Lakein quote: "Failing to plan is planning to fail." Core claim: not every business needs a formal business plan, but every successful trader needs a **trading plan** — it provides a framework, defines parameters, and maintains focus, and guards against emotional swings while trading.

Analogy: a casino gives blackjack dealers specific rules (e.g., must stay on 17, must hit below 17) to guarantee consistency and preserve the house's edge. A trading plan works the same way for a TOMIC trader — it's self-imposed but must be followed consistently. "In trading you make your own rules, but the key is to be consistent. If your rules do not work, get new ones... the only way you will know whether they work is by sticking to the plan and evaluating your performance continuously." Described as "like a flight plan and an emergency plan in one."

## The Mind-Set (heading present)

Framing analogy: a U.S. Marine going into battle trusts the process and his fellow Marines; a TOMIC manager needs the same confidence in the trading plan.

**Five required character traits for a successful TOMIC manager**, each elaborated:
1. **Dedicated** — TOMIC requires commitment through the emotional ups and downs of running the business; commitment overcomes emotional swings.
2. **Disciplined** — required to follow entries/exits and correct underwriting. Cited as the fix for the single biggest cause of blown-up accounts: **not taking losses early enough**, because the emotional pain of realizing a loss causes traders to freeze even when the plan says to cut it.
3. **Bold/decisive** — needed to act when opportunities are abundant, and equally to *not* trade when opportunities are scarce; boldness enables timely critical decisions.
4. **Flexible** — described as one of the most underrated traits. Example: shifting a portfolio from net-long to net-short when the market flips from bull to bear requires flexibility plus boldness to change course.
5. **Humble** — the market delivers "curveballs"; near-100% win rates are unrealistic. Even Warren Buffett has had bad investments (named: Salomon Brothers, US Airways, silver — bought "way before silver took off in 2010–11"). Bad trades should be treated as learning/feedback opportunities, not personal failures — "if there were no losses, no one would need to insure and then TOMIC would be out of business."

Additional emphasis on mental toughness under adverse conditions: "when a trade goes against you, be prepared but do not panic." Sports analogy: Michael Jordan, Tiger Woods — mental toughness to stay disciplined and perform under pressure. Key practice: have worst-case scenarios planned out in advance (checklist mentality), analogous to a commercial airline pilot's emergency checklist.

**Named case study: Captain Chesley B. Sullenberger**, US Airways Flight 1549 ditching in the Hudson River (January 15, 2009) after a bird strike (Canadian geese) disabled the engines. Because Sullenberger had trained for emergencies and had a plan, he executed it and saved all passengers/crew. Lesson: a trading plan gives you the scenarios mapped out in advance so you know what to do in an emergency rather than freezing.

## The Importance of Sticking to the Process (heading present)

Opens with the framing question: is it better to stick to a process, or go with your gut / be lucky vs. good? "It is better to be lucky than to be good" — but luck doesn't hold indefinitely.

**Worked blackjack example illustrating process over outcome:**
- Player has 18 (10+8), dealer shows a 6 (a "weak" dealer card — high chance the dealer has to draw and bust off an assumed 16).
- Correct process-based play: **stay** on 18, since the dealer has a good chance of busting.
- The example instead has the player **hit** (against correct strategy), draw a 3, and win with 21 — a lucky but statistically bad decision. Even though it won, "the move you made at the blackjack table was a lousy move... you won because you were lucky. But do you think that over the long run you would continue winning by making those kinds of decisions?"
- Explicit lesson: judge decisions by whether the odds justified the risk taken, not by the single-instance outcome.

Applied to TOMIC: only take trades that present a perceived winning edge; don't chase trades with no edge (i.e., don't be the blackjack player hitting on 18 "because you feel lucky"), even when it looks like others are winning without you. Over the long run, sticking to process wins more often than not.

**Process vs. outcome, explicitly stated time-horizon framing**: "Over the short term, process takes precedence over outcomes, but in the long term the outcomes take precedence over the process." I.e., short-run losses don't necessarily mean the process is wrong, but if long-run outcomes are consistently bad, the process itself must be revised.

### Checklists save lives (supporting evidence for process discipline)

Analogy: airline pilots use a preflight checklist (e.g., confirming adequate fuel) before every takeoff.

**Named medical case study**: Dr. Peter Pronovost (Johns Hopkins) developed a simple **five-item checklist** for central-line catheter insertion that dramatically reduced ICU catheter-related infections. Sourced from two cited articles (see Endnotes):
- *American Medical News*, "Infection Rates Drop as Michigan Hospitals Turn to Checklists" (March 8, 2010): catheter-related infections caused ~17,000 deaths/year nationally at the time, at ~$45,000 cost per infected patient; a group of Michigan hospitals cut catheter-related infection rates by **66%** after one year using the checklist, reaching **zero infections per 1,000 catheter-days** versus a national average of 5.2/1,000. The five checklist items: (1) wash hands, (2) use full barrier precautions when inserting the catheter, (3) clean the skin with chlorhexidine, (4) avoid certain insertion sites, (5) remove unnecessary catheters.
- *New York Times*, "A Basic Hospital To-Do List Saves Lives" (Jan. 22, 2008): over 18 months, average ICU infection rate at these hospitals fell to zero from 4%; the checklist saved **1,500+ lives and nearly $200 million**, at a program cost of only $500,000.

Applied conclusion: a checklist-driven process for investment decisions will similarly "save and make you money." TOMIC has a checklist for each function (underwriting/trade selection, risk management, trade execution — cross-referencing Ch.2–4's checklists). Framing line: "You are the casino, not the gambler" — the trader's edge comes from disciplined process, accepting that individual trades will win and lose but the process ensures winning more than losing over time.

## Questions Your Trading Plan Should Answer (heading present)

Analogy: every driver has a different vehicle/style/route but is still governed by shared traffic rules (e.g., differing speed limits by zone); similarly, a TOMIC manager sets personal "speed limits" via a trading plan that provides specific guiding parameters.

**Core list of questions every trading plan should answer:**
- What is the goal of your TOMIC?
- What markets are you going to trade?
- Which strategies are you going to use?
- What are the conditions needed to put on a trade?
- What are the conditions that will make you close a trade?
- What are your risk management parameters?
- How are you going to execute the trades?

**Expanded/follow-up questions per category** (the text explicitly encourages going into more depth than the core list):

- **Goals**: Is TOMIC meant to replace current income? Produce a consistent 20% annual return? Serve as a learning tool for options trading? Expected to always produce positive returns regardless of market conditions?
- **Strategies**: Which strategies will be used? Theta-positive only, or both theta-positive and theta-negative? Primarily iron condors, butterflies, calendars, etc.? What are your three most comfortable strategies and under what conditions do you use them? Which strategies should be avoided, and under what conditions?
- **Entry parameters**: For each chosen strategy, what specific conditions must be met to enter?
- **Exit parameters**: What winning or losing conditions trigger an exit?
- **Risk management**: What conditions trigger an adjustment? What safety limits/risk thresholds are set? (Explicitly reprises Ch.3's rules: What do you do if a position loses more than 2% of portfolio equity? What do you do if the portfolio loses more than 6% in a month?)
- **Trading journal**: What information will the journal capture? Is the recorded information sufficient for a meaningful after-action review? Will dedicated software be used?

Closing guidance: answer these to construct the plan; create it, use it, and revise it as you grow — "No trading plan is set in stone, but it is the first 'stone' that you will need to build a successful TOMIC." Explicit forward-reference: **Chapter 10, "Operating the Business: Putting Together TOMIC 1.0 from A to Z," walks through a sample trading plan** built on these questions.
