## Appendix D: Kite Spread (heading present) — captured in full as a real strategy, with construction and risk profile, per task instruction

**Definition/construction**: buy a long option *below* where an existing short spread is currently placed, then sell **more** of the original spread (now available at a higher credit, since it's being added to at a further-OTM/richer point) against that new long position — the goal being to use the added credit to pay for a substantial portion (the text's stated target: **at least half**) of the new long option's cost, reducing net cost of the adjustment.

**Character**: described as having similar characteristics to a **ratio spread**, but with **less vega and more explosive gamma.** Referenced Figure D.1 (OptionVue6 exhibit, not machine-extractable) shows a vertical call credit spread with the kite-spread adjustment overlaid, producing a risk-graph shape with a distinctive "point like a kite" at a specific price level (example given: 1390).

**Benefits** (reproduced in full):
- Long gamma; as the underlying rallies, the position goes short vega — behaving in many ways like a ratio spread.
- As time passes, the trade becomes progressively longer gamma, continuing to hedge the underlying position.
- Relatively inexpensive, hedges effectively, and produces fairly predictable returns.
- Adds much less incremental risk than the alternative of rolling the spread back and increasing size (a technique the authors explicitly disfavor — see Ch.9's iron condor adjustment discussion, where "rolling" is called out as something they are "not fans of").

**Detractions** (reproduced in full):
- There is a "sour spot" where the trade can actually lose money.
- It adds to margin requirements.
- It does not add a large amount of gamma.
- It is nearly impossible to execute effectively on the **downside** of a condor, because the trade's mechanics rely on **vertical skew** to make the added call spreads generate sufficient credit (implying it's primarily an upside/call-side adjustment tool — directly consistent with Ch.9's iron-condor-adjustment table, where the kite spread was listed as the *primary upside* adjustment).

**Execution mechanics** (worked example, reproduced): buy **1 call** below the existing spread; sell **2–3 additional call spreads** on top of/adding to the existing spread. Concrete example given (Figure D.2, OptionVue6 exhibit): **bought one 1370 call; sold three 1390/1400 call spreads.**

**Management guidance**: once placed, the recommendation is to **leave the kite spread on until the entire position is unwound together** — it's cheap enough as an adjustment that it's worth carrying through to the end of the trade rather than actively trading in and out of it.
