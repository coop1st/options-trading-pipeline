## Overview / Introduction (p.88)

Opens with a Darwin quote: "It is not the strongest of the species that survives, nor the most intelligent that survives. It is the one that is the most adaptable to change."

Core claim: a successful TOMIC manager must learn and adapt as conditions change. Every successful business receives and acts on feedback (from customers, employees, competitors, suppliers) to continuously improve; a TOMIC manager needs an equivalent embedded learning process. **Three named elements of TOMIC's learning process**: a trading journal, a sounding board, and a continuing education plan — these map directly to the chapter's three main sections.

## The Trading Journal (heading present)

Good records are a prerequisite for successfully operating TOMIC — they provide feedback and let the business "keep score." Analogy: American football teams keep score and tape games to analyze what worked/didn't (example: the Dallas Cowboys preparing for games using prior-game film).

Argument for persistence via feedback: durable companies (named: **Coca-Cola, GE, Apple**) survive long-term because they adapt strategy based on market feedback. **Named case study: "New Coke."** Coca-Cola's reformulation was a disaster — sales plummeted and competitors gained share — but because Coca-Cola listened to consumer feedback, it reintroduced "Coke Classic." Second analogy: airplane "black boxes" exist purely for feedback (to prevent recurrence of the same failure), not for dramatic reporting.

**"The trading diary is your black box recorder"** — designed to provide feedback and improve the business. Cited reference for trading-journal practice: Dr. Alexander Elder's book *Come into My Trading Room: A Complete Guide to Trading* (previously referenced in Ch.3 as Dennis's trading instructor).

**Table 7.1 — "Data to Capture in Your Trading Log"** is referenced as the recommended per-trade data set (table contents not extractable as literal text from the PDF beyond the table caption — the surrounding narrative indicates it should include enough data to measure the manager's effectiveness; readers are told they may add further fields of their own). Recommended practice: maintain this as a real-time-linked Excel spreadsheet to monitor open TOMIC positions.

**Recommended cumulative-analysis cadence**: analyze results monthly, quarterly, and annually; pick a benchmark index for relative-performance comparison.

**Trading-journal review questions** (explicitly listed):
1. How many trades did you enter this month, quarter, and year?
2. How many trades were profitable?
3. How many were losing trades?
4. What was your win rate?
5. What were your average days in the trade?
6. How much was your average win?
7. How much was your average loss?
8. How much did you win or lose on average per trade?
9. What is your average yield (realized profit / margin used) per trade?

**Worked example — Table 7.2, "Example of the Monthly Trade Analysis at TOMIC"** (metrics narrated in text even though the table itself isn't machine-extractable):
- 9 losing trades, total losses $(15,445), average loss size $(1,716).
- Win ratio (% winning trades) = **94%**.
- Total capital risked = **$1,833,878**.
- Average days in trade (DIT) = **26**.
- Average monthly yield = **3.6%** (computed as $66,417 / $1,833,878).
- **Annualized yield from that monthly figure = 63.8%** (see the annualization formula in Endnote 1 below — this is how the 63.8% is actually derived, not via naive multiplication).

**Illustrative sensitivity example — win rate isn't everything on its own:** if win rate fell from 94% to 90%, is that necessarily worse? Not necessarily — the text works through a comparison (Tables 7.3 vs. 7.4) where at 94% win rate the $loss/$win ratio is 3.2, but at 90% win rate the $loss/$win ratio falls to 1.0 (e.g., average loss $(500) vs. average win $500) — and the **actual expected payout per trade increases from $0.75 to $0.80** despite the lower win rate. Lesson: judge performance using the joint combination of win rate *and* the loss/win size ratio (i.e., expected value), not win rate alone.

**Tying the journal back to the trading plan**: the journal lets you check actual performance against plan targets. In the Table 7.2 example, TOMIC's stated goal was 2%–4% monthly return; realizing 3.6% confirms the goal is being hit, providing the manager confidence; missing the goal would instead be a signal to adjust the trading approach.

## Sounding Board (heading present)

Trading is described as "a lonely profession"; the manager needs to stay grounded in reality. Since underwriting/trade-selection skill is a key TOMIC success factor (per Ch.1's success drivers), improving it requires being open to outside feedback. **Two named ways to build a feedback system**: (1) join a trading group, (2) hire a trading coach.

### Joining a Trading Group (sub-heading present)

Explicitly debunks a common misconception: watching CNBC talking heads is *not* being part of a trading group. A real trading group is a sounding board for new ideas and provides accountability (reinforcing adherence to your own trading plan), even though you remain ultimately responsible for your own trades. Recommendation: find a group with a trading style/background similar to your own.

**Questions to ask when choosing a trading group** (reproduced in full):
- What are the goals of the trading group?
- How will interactions happen — in person, online, by phone? Via a chat tool (Skype, AIM, Google Talk, etc.)?
- How often will the group meet?
- How many members?
- What are the members' experience levels?
- If everyone is a novice, is this "the blind leading the blind"?
- If experience levels vary, will more experienced members actually share with less experienced ones?
- What do *you* bring to the group?
- Does the group keep a meeting log? (If so, review past logs to see what's been covered.)
- Do members pool resources?
- Does the group have a coach?

### Hiring a Trading Coach (sub-heading present)

A trading coach listens to your strategies, holds you accountable, and helps you continuously improve, drawing on greater experience.

**Questions to ask when hiring a trading coach** (reproduced in full):
- What can I learn from this coach?
- Can the coach provide references? (Talk to prior clients.)
- How available is the coach — how easily can you reach them if you get in trouble?
- How much weekly interaction time will you get?
- What communication medium will be used? (Common example given: online meeting tools like WebEx or GoToMeeting.)
- Does the coach know enough to actually challenge you and push improvement?
- Is the coach an individual, or part of a larger coaching organization?
- Does the coach run "student" trading groups you could join?
- Does the coach still actively trade real money — and how is their performance?
- How good are the coach's communication skills?

## Continuing Education (heading present)

Framing: every skilled profession (explicitly named: medical doctors, dentists, lawyers, computer engineers) requires ongoing continuing education via seminars, classes, etc. TOMIC managers should do the same to stay current in options trading.

Analogy: a karate practitioner who only knows blocking and punching can defend themselves adequately, but learning to kick adds more options for causing damage to an opponent — similarly, a TOMIC manager should be fluent across the full range of option strategies and skilled at reading market conditions to select the best-fit strategy, continuously learning.

**Suggested education-plan components**: reading books, attending seminars, taking specialized classes, or joining a coaching service — **OptionPit.com is named explicitly** as an example coaching service. Explicit cross-references: **Appendix A ("Recommended Reading")** for book recommendations, and **Appendix C ("OptionPit.com")** for more on that service.

## Endnote (heading present)

1. **Correct annualized-yield formula** (explicitly called out as a common point of error): most people incorrectly annualize by dividing the period yield by the number of days held and multiplying by 365. **The correct Excel formula given**: 

`(1 + yield)^(365.25 / number_of_days) - 1`

This is the formula underlying the 63.8% annualized figure derived from the 3.6% monthly yield / 26 average days-in-trade example above.
